# T009 — how the cockpit keeps records

Decision paper, revised once after adversary attack. Seat: option-maker, team T009, mission M001. Scratch, not authoritative. Written against `notepad/decision-2026-09-19/question.md`, the commander's advice in DS002 and DS003, their rulings D020 and D021, the auditor's evidence, the scout's reading of official guidance, and sixteen adversary findings.

## The problem in five lines

The pilot forgets everything between sessions and can learn only from files, yet of twelve records drafted in two days the commander kept one whole, and the four that landed earlier are the only store in the cockpit ever cited twice.
Both attempts wrote before the need was felt, from one thin source, so the commander became the filter and paid in reading time.
Rule 2's per-claim sourcing forced the commander's own conversational fragments into the prose, and the annotations they left in two drafts show what that costs: a required principle reduced to the one clause they happened to quote, spitballing presented as canon, an argument reproduced word for word with its point missing.
Nothing in the cockpit routes the pilot to a record at the moment it would help; records are opened at the pilot's discretion, and discretion has produced two citations in four days.
The commander's vision is under heavy construction, so knowledge about flightcrew has a short half-life while knowledge about the harness does not.

## What the evidence settles, and what it does not

**Drift has not produced records, and the reason is partly circular.** The auditor lists eight bullets; two of them share one root cause and one repair, and one covers two separate rejections, so the underlying events are eight but not the eight the bullets suggest. Every one was repaired by a rule, a dossier line or a memory, and none by a record. The honest qualification, which I owe: no record-producing trigger existed in that window, and rule 2 required a team plus a commander pre-read before anything could land, so "none by a record" measures the absence of a cheap path as much as the unsuitability of the trigger. Two of the events were doctrinal — the records-and-scratch conflation and the two rejected writing cycles — so my claim that this cockpit's drift is not yet doctrinal is withdrawn. What survives is narrower and still decisive: drift so far has produced conduct corrections and one doctrine of record-keeping, and the fastest vehicle for both is a rule or a procedure.

**The records that work are the ones nobody mined.** The four `records/claude-code/` files are the only store with repeat cross-session citation: one caught a false claim, one settled a live dispute. The commander's reasons for what they did with the twelve are their own and are not about quote density: the four stubs were ruled on timing, because those topics "are probably going to pop up 'on demand' anyway"; the two drops were ruled on relevance, as "old and irrelevant ramblings"; the register was kept on quality and shape, "decent include as is". That external-fact records survive and commander-mined ones do not is this paper's hypothesis, not the commander's finding, and the confound the commander themselves names is at least as good an explanation: harness knowledge is stable and flightcrew knowledge is under construction.

**Official guidance supports the trigger in part, not whole.** Of the memory page's four cases for writing something down, two are drift — the same mistake a second time, and the same correction typed as last session. The third is a review catch, which this cockpit has no analogue for outside the commander. The fourth, that a new teammate would need the same context, is anticipatory onboarding, which is writing before the need is felt and is the practice this paper argues against. Two of four, claimed as two of four.

**Official routing keeps constant rules where they are.** The memory page's full instruction is to keep the always-loaded file to "facts Claude should hold in every session: build commands, conventions, project layout, 'always do X' rules", and only then: "If an entry is a multi-step procedure or only matters for one part of the codebase, move it to a skill or a path-scoped rule instead." Only two narrow classes move out. A one-line always-do-X rule such as never deleting an unmerged branch, or always Opus for an adversary, stays in `CLAUDE.md`, and the same page warns that a rule behind an unmatched trigger is simply absent. My claim that the always-loaded file is on a bad trajectory is withdrawn: at most four of its eighteen rules trace to drift, two drift repairs landed outside it entirely, and the bulk arrived in one commander-ordered rewrite, which is design rather than growth.

**The index-plus-topic-files shape is confirmed three times over,** by auto memory, the subagent `memory` field and the multi-agent research post, capped at 200 lines or 25KB with detail in files opened on demand. `records/README.md` plus `records/*.md` is already that shape. The structure was never the problem.

**The cost driver is teams, not files.** Four times chat tokens, up to fifteen for multi-agent, is the cost of a team writing a record, not of keeping one. Two teams were spent to land one document whole.

## The options

Each is a whole method: trigger, writer, sources, form, validation, currency, delivery, cost.

**A — shelve record keeping.** No trigger; `records/` freezes at four files; the drafts stay in the notepad as source material. Knowledge travels by the mission file, the session log, `orders.json` and `CLAUDE.md`. One decision, then nothing. It cannot go stale and it cannot help, and it leaves rule 2 as a docspec for a practice nobody performs.

**B — on-demand drift records with team validation.** A drift event opens a record; a small team writes it from the wide net with the topic store last; plain register; a one-line stamp; a later drift reopens it. Delivery by a manifest line. One read per record for the commander. Sound in itself, but its trigger has fired eight times and produced conduct rules, so it would mostly idle.

**C — stubs now, records on contact.** B plus a stub tier written up front and promoted on contact. The commander has already ruled the four stubs in D020, so stage one is settled fact rather than a live choice; as a standing method it still writes before use.

**D — route knowledge by vehicle.** The first question is which vehicle carries the knowledge: a constant always-do-X rule in `CLAUDE.md`; a damage-preventing rule in the PreToolUse guard, which enforces rather than instructs; a multi-step routine in `procedures/` behind a manifest line; a convention in a path-scoped rule; a procedure crew perform in a skill; the mission file; the session log; the crew dossier; and `records/` for durable facts, chiefly external ones. Low cost per item. Without a trigger, nothing starts, which is how the cockpit arrived here twice.

**E — vehicle routing with a drift trigger, on demand and periodically.** The drift event opens a question about vehicle, not a record. The pilot classifies and writes on the spot unless the vehicle is a record, which dispatches crew. The method lives in `procedures/record-keeping.md`; the manifest carries the triggers; a recurring currency review supplies the periodic leg.

**F — the harness's own memory.** Auto memory is the closest official precedent to this problem, and the pilot already has one on this machine: a `MEMORY.md` index of six one-line entries loaded at every session start, topic files behind it, holding exactly the conduct corrections the auditor listed, including branch cleanup as merge cleanup and adversaries checking the pilot's work. Trigger is agent-judged usefulness to a future session; the writer is the pilot itself; form is capped and self-pruning under a harness nag; delivery is guaranteed at session start; cost to the commander is near zero and no team is dispatched. Against it: it is machine-local, outside git, outside the cockpit and invisible to the commander's review, so it cannot hold what the commander must govern; it duplicates the commander's dossier, where the same branch rule already lives; and it cuts against rule 1's principle that durable knowledge lives in the cockpit. It is not a replacement for records, and it is a real and already-running vehicle for the pilot's own conduct, which is what the drift list is mostly made of.

**G — enforcement instead of instruction.** The docs say memory is context, not enforced configuration, and that to block an action regardless of what the model decides you use a PreToolUse hook. The cockpit already runs one. For the one drift event that was a near-miss rather than a correction — eleven branches about to be deleted — no record, rule, procedure or memory would have stopped the command; the guard would have. As a whole method it covers only the small class of actions that can be mechanically detected and are worth blocking, so it is not a record-keeping method. As a vehicle it is the strongest one available, and it was missing from the first draft.

## The comparison

B and C fail on their own terms: their trigger has fired and produced rules. A and D survive for opposite reasons, A because flightcrew record production should stop, D because the knowledge still has to go somewhere. F covers conduct cheaply but cannot hold what the commander must govern. G covers damage and nothing else. Only D and E route, and only E starts.
On delivery, the case is narrower than I first made it. I withdraw "rule 4 failed": rule 4 is itself a condition line, no post-rule-4 instance of it being ignored exists, and the two records it was said to point at are named by the crew-protocol line, not by rule 4. Session logs record what happened, not what was read, so they cannot establish that a file went unopened. The manifest's advantage over a `CLAUDE.md` condition line is therefore a hypothesis, and it is testable: the pilot logs each time a trigger line sent it to a file, and any drift event a manifest line should have caught counts against it.
On the commander's cost, A is zero, F near zero, D and G low per item, E low and lumpy, B lumpy, C already absorbed.

## The recommendation

**Option E, amended, with F and G adopted as vehicles inside it.** The drift trigger becomes the cockpit's general rule for writing anything down, and what it opens is a question about vehicle. On the evidence the answer will usually be a procedure, sometimes a one-line rule, sometimes the guard, sometimes the pilot's own memory, and least often a record. `records/` stays open and expects external-fact records and the occasional validated local one.

Stop writing records about the system, write procedures about the job, enforce what can be enforced, and let the manifest and the periodic review make both readable.

**One reversal the commander should rule on knowingly.** DS002 asked for the commander's statements to be mined, stored and converted into vision records, "periodically conducted to pick up more commanders knowledge". DS003 changes the timing to on demand while keeping mining itself "very useful for fine tuning documents and updating them due to the system drifting". This paper follows DS003: no team mines for new records again. The topic store is kept and read as a fine-tuning source when an existing record or procedure is revised, which is the use DS003 names. If the commander meant DS003 to narrow the timing only, and not to retire record production from mining, the recommendation should be re-weighted toward B.

## What changes

**Rule 2 becomes a docspec, and the sourcing test survives in changed form.** Nothing about sourcing is dropped. Every claim in a record still has a source; what changes is where that source is recorded — per claim in the writer's working notes, and named by kind in the header — and that no source is named in the prose. A record carries one header line and no citations or quotations in the body: `Source:` with URL, date and researcher for a web-sourced record, as the four existing records already do; `Validated: checked against the commander's directives, the flightdeck library and the source guides; T011 validator, 2026-09-19.` for a locally sourced one, naming the kinds of source checked, the team and seat that checked them, and the date, with no file names, line numbers or topic ids. The team id is one token, not the reference block the commander objected to, and it resolves to exactly one entry in `dispatch/cockpit.json`; it is a point of taste and the fallback, if they want no identifier at all, is the date and seat alone. The test is kept and its expression moves from the prose to the header, enforced by the validator before the line is applied. There is no ledger file; crew rule 7 already records the team, its outcome and its lessons in `dispatch/<room>.json`, findable by the stamp's date. Rule 2 states three further things about the line so none of them can be missed: the seat that applies it did not write the document and the pilot may never stamp its own work; an in-place edit voids the stamp, so the record is re-validated or the line is deleted; and the stamp is not a substitute for the commander's read before a record lands. Rule 2 also gains one line placing the vehicle test before the six tests, and one permitting a record to land as a stub. The line caps stay at 100 and 150: nothing in the evidence shows length caused any failure, and the exemplar the rule itself names runs to 74 lines.

**The top line carries two axes, not one.** Maturity first: while a file is a stub it carries `Stub: existing knowledge only, not validated, not to be cited as authority.` and that line stands alone, whatever its sources, because a stub is by the commander's own definition a brief mention of existing knowledge awaiting improvement and a stamp on one would be false on its face. On promotion the stub line is removed and replaced by what the record has earned. Sourcing second: `Source:` for a web-sourced record, naming URL, date and researcher; `Validated:` for a locally sourced one, as above; both lines, `Source:` first, for a record drawing on both. Procedures carry neither: their authority is the commander's approval of their manifest line, which is the same authority by a cheaper route. The existing local `Source:` form in `records/README.md` line 5, with topic ids, commander turns and advice paths, is not reused for the stamp: that is the form the commander read in the drafts and called a jumbled mess of number-based references, so the stamp names kinds of source, a seat and a date, and carries no identifiers at all.

**The docspec lives in two files and both change together.** `records/README.md` line 3 states that the header's topic or notepad pointer and per-claim sources are both required. Per-claim sourcing is not dropped there either; the line is rewritten to say what it now means: every claim still has a source, recorded per claim in the writer's working notes and named by kind in the header, with no pointer and no identifier in the file itself. Line 5 defines the three `Source:` forms and the inline-claim rule; the web form stays as it is, the local form is replaced by the `Validated:` stamp, and the sentence requiring an inline citation where a claim is not obvious from the header goes. The README also gains one line saying how a stub is indexed, since it is not a record and the index has never had to carry one. Without this the index would contradict the docspec, and the README's own tie-break only covers a record disagreeing with the docspec, not the README disagreeing with it.

**The validator is one Opus seat, not the writer.** It opens the sources itself, in full, never the writer's extracts, because choosing the fragment is the failure being tested for. Of each statement it asks whether the named sources say this, and whether this is a fair reading of what they say in full. Unsupported statements are deleted; an unfair reading goes back to the writer once, with the fuller passage, which requires the writing seat to still be alive under crew rule 6. The pilot sees the deletions and the returns before the line is applied. Stripping the anchors out of the prose does make this check harder, and the cost is paid off the page rather than on it: the writer hands the validator its working notes, each statement against the source passage it came from, as a scratch file. The validator uses them as a map and still opens every source in full, which is what keeps one seat sufficient rather than turning the check into a re-derivation of the whole corpus. The notes are filed in the team's notepad folder with its reports and never in the record, and are not cleared while the record stands; git history is the backstop. They claim no exception to rule 3: the notepad is scratch, the notes are an aid to re-checking and not an authority, the authority is the record and its stamp, and a re-validation can rebuild the map if they are gone. The limit, stated plainly: the wider net is the primary protection against a fair-reading failure and the stamp is the second, because the net gives the writer the whole principle instead of a fragment.

**Procedures get the same eight-part treatment, kept light.** A procedure states its trigger condition and its steps and nothing else; thirty lines working, fifty capped; written by the pilot on the spot; sourced from the wide net where it states a system fact and from the commander's order where it states conduct; checked by the mini-adversary roster only when structural; kept current by editing in place on the next drift and swept by the periodic review; delivered by one manifest line the commander approves. That approval is the commander's input into every procedure, and the one check in this method they actually see.

**`procedures/record-keeping.md` is written first,** carrying the drift definition, the vehicle test, who writes each vehicle, the validator's question and what the commander sees.

**Delivery.** The manifest is imported into the cockpit `CLAUDE.md`, and this is measured rather than read: on CLI 2.1.275, a subdirectory `CLAUDE.md` importing `@proc/triggers.md` which imports `@deeper.md` delivered both as instruction blocks in two runs, with the session inside that subdirectory and with it starting in the parent, relative paths resolving against the importing file. Two hops proven, four documented, one needed; the external-import approval dialog and a fifth hop are untested and nothing rests on them. A records index line may summarise, which is the official shape and resolves the contradiction between this paper's two halves; a manifest line states a condition, because it is an instruction to act and not a description of a file. The manifest has no enforced cap and I withdraw the invented forty; what actually limits it is that every line needs the commander's approval. Against a skill: a skill is outside the cockpit and so a rule 6 change, its body enters context as a snapshot not re-read on later turns, and its matching is a model judgement; propose skills through `base/` once the procedure set stabilises. Against a hook: a hook is stronger than both, and belongs wherever an action can be detected and should be blocked, which is why the branch rule goes to the guard rather than behind a trigger.

**Constant rules stay in `CLAUDE.md`.** Only genuinely multi-step material leaves. The dispatch procedure absorbs the steps of forming a team; the one-line rules it rests on — Opus for judgement and for adversaries, keep teammates alive while the team works — stay where they are.

**The periodic leg, which is also how a local record stays current.** A web record's currency is mechanical: its `Source:` line names a URL and a date, and it is re-researched when the page moves. A local record has no such artefact, so its stamp does the work instead. The stamp's date and the kinds of source it names are the hook, and the recurring currency review is the mechanism: it re-validates any local record whose named source kinds have changed since that date — a new commander directive, a library revision, a manual rewritten — and retires what can no longer be earned. The drift trigger is the second path: a record that misled is reopened on the spot, without waiting for the review. The review is itself a procedure and is specified like one. Owner: the pilot. Cadence: at the close of a launch and at the close of a mission phase, not by calendar. Mechanism, cheapest first: for the library, the manuals and the source guides, a git log on those paths since the stamp date shows mechanically whether a named source kind has moved; for commander directives, new entries in `orders.json` and `decisions.json` since that date do the same. Only a record whose sources actually moved goes to a validator. Cost when nothing has moved: nothing. The same pass reads the topic store for fine-tuning where a document has drifted and retires stale entries, and it is logged in the maintenance JSON the commander asked about in DS002, alongside the library and terms items already open in the workshop. This restores the second half of the founding note, which says the pilot reads "on demand or periodically".

**The seven drafts.** The register lands in `records/claude-code/` as is. The four ruled stubs are written at fifteen lines working and twenty capped, plain register, zero commander quotes, by one seat working from the drafts and the wide net; the commander's inline comments are context for the four sections they touch and not a complete edit list, so the writer still casts the net. The two dropped drafts stay in the notepad. No fifth stub is written ahead of a drift event.

**One either-or for the commander, not a decision this paper takes.** Rule 2 ends "the commander reads a record before it lands". The four stubs are new fifteen-line compressions, not the annotated drafts, so landing them unread would amend that clause. Either amend it to exempt anything marked as a stub, or keep the read. **I recommend keeping the read.** Sixty lines of plain prose across four files is the cheapest gate this system has, DS003 ruled disposition and never waived review, and the clause is the last commander-visible check on `records/`. The case for amending is that any read at all is the filtering cost this decision exists to remove.

**What the commander does per item.** A record: one read and a yes or no on usefulness, with no filtering for relevance, because the drift event proved it first. A procedure: approve one manifest line. A stub: one short read, on the recommendation above. Standing cost: occasionally saying that a correction was costly, which is the strongest of the four drift signals.

**The first outputs, given the drift list, are not records.** First `procedures/record-keeping.md`, from the two rejected writing cycles. Second a dispatch procedure, absorbing the multi-step part of three drift events about crew models, understaffed judgement and premature shutdown, while their one-line rules stay put. Third the branch rule into the guard, where it is enforced rather than remembered. The first genuine record candidate arrives when a launch meets the harness and something documented turns out to be wrong.

## Risks, and what would make this wrong

Most checks in this method are performed by the party being checked. The pilot judges its own drift, names its own vehicle, writes the log and writes the crew briefs. One check has an input the commander actually sees, the manifest line they approve for every procedure; the rest are self-assessment, and I state that rather than dress three pilot-run instruments as controls. The strongest external signal available is the commander marking a correction costly.
Routing can become a way to write nothing, with every gap declared a procedure and the procedure never written.
A rule behind a trigger is absent whenever the trigger does not fire, which is the documented failure mode and the reason damage-preventing rules go to the guard and constant rules stay in the always-loaded file.
The stamp attests that each statement has a source and is a fair reading of it; it does not make any single claim traceable to its source on the page. That traceability is traded off the page deliberately, under D021, because it was paid for on every read by every agent in prose quality and cashed in almost never. It is not destroyed: the writer's working notes hold it in the notepad. What is genuinely lost is traceability for a reader who has only the record in front of them, and the recovery is to open the notes or re-validate, which is one seat.
A stamp is only as good as the seat that applies it, and the seats are the pilot's to choose. Rule 2 forbids the pilot stamping its own work, but the pilot still picks the validator, briefs it and reads its verdict, so this control is weaker than an independent audit and stronger than nothing.
The manifest's delivery advantage over a condition line in `CLAUDE.md` is unproven and is carried as a hypothesis with a stated measure.

It is wrong if a launch produces doctrine drift fast enough that records become the dominant output rather than the residue; the evidence would be three drift events inside one launch each resolving to a record, and the method should re-weight toward B.
It is wrong if the commander meant DS002's periodic mining into records to stand, in which case the reversal named above is the thing to reject.
It is wrong if the pilot's vehicle classification proves unreliable, for which the only real measure is the commander overruling a manifest line twice.
It is wrong, and A is right whole, if construction goes far enough to rebuild the cockpit, at which point even procedures are short-lived and the correct move is to stop writing and keep flying.
