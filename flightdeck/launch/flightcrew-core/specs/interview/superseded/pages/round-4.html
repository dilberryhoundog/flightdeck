The intent node says why flightcrew exists and what it is. It does not say what this second attempt leaves behind, or why the first attempt's result is not it. Ground: the built system is 12,579 lines, 68% runner and check libraries; its 30 suites pass at HEAD; the sources put the human at three gates reading evidence and writing the diagnosis afterwards. The ten registered problems name what is wrong. One sentence fixes what the end-to-end proof exercises and what the critic judges the diff against.

Q1What is the outcome of this run, in one sentence?

This becomes the intent's outcome clause. The test-builder derives the end-to-end proof from it and the planner decides what the last wave must leave behind. Each option makes a different thing primary.

Q2Why is the first attempt's result not the outcome? Pick the reason the intent should carry.

The intent's why clause. The run log, the plan's risks and the critic's reading all lean on this sentence when deciding whether a defect is in scope. The three options are the three roots the problem set traces to; only one can be the headline.

Q3At the end of this run, what must you be able to do that you cannot today?

Acceptance is written last but it is anchored here: this is the thing the owner confirms. The options differ in how much of a real run must happen in front of you.

Q4What is this run's stance toward what is built and green today?

Decides whether the spec is written as a revision of an existing system or as a definition the existing files must be made to fit. It shapes every scope node: what the implementers may keep, what they must not rebuild, and what the critic treats as pre-existing.
